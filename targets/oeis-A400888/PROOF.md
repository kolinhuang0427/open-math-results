# Proof of Surjectivity of OEIS A400888 onto the Positive Integers

## Primary Source and Statement

From **OEIS A400888** (author: M. Jeremie Lafitte (Levitas), Oct 08 2026; revision 16, offset `0,1`, last modified `2026-10-15T16:25:59-04:00`):

- **Name:** *"Contents of the quarter-turn corrected Stern vectors associated with the column words of the Gosper-Lafitte triangle."*
- **Definition (`COMMENT` and `FORMULA`):**
  For $n \ge 0$, let $V(n) = (v_0, v_1, \dots, v_{q_0 - 1}) \in \{0, 1\}^{q_0}$ be the top-to-bottom binary column word of column $n$ of the Gosper–Lafitte triangle (`A000360`, rev 154), which is the reversal of the word $W(n)$ in `A000361` (rev 53), with $f_n = \mathrm{A000360}(n)$ ones and $e_n = \mathrm{A284556}(n+1)$ zeros. Starting with $(p, q) = (1, 1)$, read $V(n)$ from left to right, replacing $(p, q)$ by $(p + q, q)$ after a `1` and by $(p, p + q)$ after a `0`, producing $(p_n, q_n)$. Then
  $$a(n) = \gcd(p_n - e_n,\, q_n + f_n).$$
- **Explicit column-word characterizations:**
  1. In `A400888` (`PROGRAM`), $V(n)$ is generated by the PARI/GP recurrence
     $$f(v, c, h) = [\,2(x + h) + c - (2c + 1)(x \bmod 2) \mid x \in v\,],$$
     $$w(0) = [1], \quad w(2m + 1) = f\bigl(w(m),\, [m \not\equiv 2 \pmod 3],\, 1\bigr),$$
     $$w(2m) = f\bigl(w(m),\, [m \not\equiv 0 \pmod 3],\, 0\bigr) \,\Vert\, f\bigl(w(m - 1),\, [m \not\equiv 2 \pmod 3],\, 0\bigr) \quad (m \ge 1),$$
     where $V(n)$ is obtained by sorting $w(n)$ in descending order and taking each entry modulo $2$.
  2. In `A000361` (`COMMENT` and `FORMULA`), setting $p_0 = \mathrm{A002487}(n)$ and $q_0 = \mathrm{A002487}(n + 1)$ (Stern's diatomic sequence `A002487`, rev 719: $\mathrm{fusc}(0)=0$, $\mathrm{fusc}(1)=1$, $\mathrm{fusc}(2m)=\mathrm{fusc}(m)$, $\mathrm{fusc}(2m+1)=\mathrm{fusc}(m)+\mathrm{fusc}(m+1)$), the word $V(n) = (v_0, \dots, v_{q_0-1})$ is given by
     $$v_j = \bigl(((j p_0 - 1) \bmod q_0) + 1 - p_0\bigr) \bmod 2 \qquad (0 \le j < q_0).$$
- **Conjecture (`A400888`, `COMMENT`):**
  > *"Every positive integer divides some term of this unbounded sequence. Every positive even integer occurs infinitely often as a term. It is conjectured that the sequence is surjective onto the positive integers."*

---

## Main Theorem

**Theorem.** *Every positive integer $r \ge 1$ occurs infinitely many times as a value of $\mathrm{A400888}(n)$. In particular, $\mathrm{A400888} : \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0} \to \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1}$ is surjective.*

The proof proceeds via an explicit two-parameter index family
$$n(r, t) := 2^{2t + r - 1} - 2^{r - 1} - 2 \qquad (r \ge 2,\; t \ge 1).$$
First (Lemma 1), we prove directly from **both** the PARI recurrence $w(n)$ and the `A000361` modular formula that $V(n(r, t)) = (0^r 1^r)^{t - 1} 0^r 1^{r - 1}$. Second (Lemma 2), we evaluate $a(n(r, t)) = r \gcd(X_t(r), Z_t(r))$ and establish a quadratic-form invariance identity forcing
$$\gcd\bigl(X_t(r), Z_t(r)\bigr) \;\Big|\; t \cdot \bigl((t - 1) r - 2\bigr).$$
Third (Lemma 3), $n = 2$ and $t \in \{1, 2, 3\}$ settle $r = 1$, all even $r \ge 2$, and all odd $r \ge 3$ except $r \equiv 27 \pmod{30}$ completely elementarily. Fourth (Lemmas 4–6), for every odd $r \ge 3$ divisible by $3$, choosing $t = 2u + 1$ with $p = r u - 1$ prime in a fixed arithmetic progression yields $(t - 1) r - 2 = 2p$; an exact algebraic norm bound rules out $p \mid \gcd(X_t, Z_t)$ for all large $p$, while the Prime Number Theorem in Arithmetic Progressions and the Brun–Titchmarsh inequality show that a positive proportion of such primes have no prime divisor $q \mid t$ dividing $\gcd(X_t, Z_t)$, giving infinitely many $t$ with $a(n(r, t)) = r$.

## Lemma 1: Column Words for $n(r, t)$ and Periodicity $V(4n + 3) = V(n)$

**Lemma 1.**
1. *For every $n \ge 0$, $V(4n + 3) = V(n)$, and hence $a(4^j(n + 1) - 1) = a(n)$ for all $j \ge 0$.*
2. *For all integers $r \ge 2$ and $t \ge 1$, let $n(r, t) := 2^{2t + r - 1} - 2^{r - 1} - 2$. Then both the PARI recurrence $w(n)$ of `A400888` and the modular coding formula of `A000361` yield*
   $$V\bigl(n(r, t)\bigr) = \bigl(0^r \, 1^r\bigr)^{t - 1} \, 0^r \, 1^{r - 1},$$
   *with $e_{n(r, t)} = t r$ zeros and $f_{n(r, t)} = t r - 1$ ones.*

### Proof

Write each nonnegative integer uniquely as $x = 2y + b$ with $y = \lfloor x/2 \rfloor \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}$ and $b = x \bmod 2 \in \{0, 1\}$. For any $c \in \{0, 1\}$ and $h \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}$,
$$2(x + h) + c - (2c + 1)(x \bmod 2) = 2(2y + h) + (b + c - 2bc) = 2(2y + h) + (b \oplus c),$$
where $\oplus$ denotes addition modulo $2$. Representing $w(n)$ by the multiset $S(n) := \{(\lfloor x/2 \rfloor, x \bmod 2) : x \in w(n)\}$, the recurrence becomes
- $S(0) = \{(0, 1)\}$,
- $S(2m + 1) = \{(2y + 1,\, b \oplus [m \not\equiv 2 \pmod 3]) : (y, b) \in S(m)\}$ ($m \ge 0$),
- $S(2m) = \{(2y,\, b \oplus [m \not\equiv 0 \pmod 3]) : (y, b) \in S(m)\} \sqcup \{(2y,\, b \oplus [m \not\equiv 2 \pmod 3]) : (y, b) \in S(m - 1)\}$ ($m \ge 1$).

**Proof of (1).** Applying the odd rule twice from $n$ to $2n + 1$ to $4n + 3$ maps each $(y, b) \in S(n)$ to $(4y + 3,\, b \oplus c_1 \oplus c_2)$, where $c_1 = [n \not\equiv 2 \pmod 3]$ and $c_2 = [2n + 1 \not\equiv 2 \pmod 3]$. Since $2n + 1 \equiv 2 \pmod 3 \iff n \equiv 2 \pmod 3$, we have $c_1 = c_2$ and $c_1 \oplus c_2 = 0$. The resulting map on entries $x = 2y + b \mapsto 8y + 6 + b$ is strictly increasing ($8y + 6 < 8y + 7 < 8(y + 1) + 6$) and preserves parity, so sorting in descending order yields $V(4n + 3) = V(n)$. (In `A000361`, $\mathrm{fusc}(4n+3) = \mathrm{fusc}(n) + 2\,\mathrm{fusc}(n+1)$ and $\mathrm{fusc}(4n+4) = \mathrm{fusc}(n+1)$, and $v_j$ depends only on $p_0 \bmod 2q_0$, giving the same identity.) Iterating $n \mapsto 4n + 3$ gives $a(4^j(n + 1) - 1) = a(n)$ for all $j \ge 0$.

**Proof of (2) from `w(n)`.** Observe that if all first coordinates $y$ in $S(n)$ are distinct, then $2y + b > 2y' + b' \iff y > y'$, so sorting $w(n)$ in descending order sorts $S(n)$ strictly by descending $y$. We establish four short inductions:

- **Step 1:** $S(2^k - 1) = \{(2^k - 1,\, (k + 1) \bmod 2)\}$ for all $k \ge 0$.
  At $k = 0$, $S(0) = \{(0, 1)\}$. For $k \to k + 1$, $2^{k+1} - 1 = 2m + 1$ with $m = 2^k - 1 \equiv 0 \text{ or } 1 \pmod 3$, so $[m \not\equiv 2 \pmod 3] = 1$, sending $(2^k - 1, (k + 1) \bmod 2)$ to $(2^{k+1} - 1, (k + 2) \bmod 2)$.

- **Step 2:** $S(2^k - 2) = \{(2^k - 2^j,\, j \bmod 2) : 1 \le j \le k\}$ for all $k \ge 1$.
  At $k = 1$, $S(0) = \{(0, 1)\}$. For $k \to k + 1$, $2^{k+1} - 2 = 2m$ with $m = 2^k - 1$. Here $[m \not\equiv 2 \pmod 3] = 1$ and $[m \not\equiv 0 \pmod 3] = [2^k \equiv 2 \pmod 3] = k \bmod 2$. Thus $S(m)$ contributes $(2^{k+1} - 2,\, ((k + 1) + k) \bmod 2) = (2^{k+1} - 2^1,\, 1)$, while $S(m - 1) = S(2^k - 2)$ contributes $\{(2^{k+1} - 2^{j+1},\, (j + 1) \bmod 2) : 1 \le j \le k\}$. Their union is $\{(2^{k+1} - 2^j,\, j \bmod 2) : 1 \le j \le k + 1\}$.

- **Step 3:** Fix $t \ge 1$ and set $M(r, t) := 2^{2t + r - 2} - 2^{r - 2} - 1$ for $r \ge 2$. Then $M(r, t) \equiv 2 \pmod 3$ and
  $$S\bigl(M(r, t)\bigr) = \bigl\{\bigl(2^{2t + r - 2} - 2^{r - 2 + j} + 2^{r - 2} - 1,\; j \bmod 2\bigr) : 1 \le j \le 2t\bigr\}.$$
  At $r = 2$, $M(2, t) = 4^t - 2 \equiv 2 \pmod 3$, and Step 2 ($k = 2t$) gives the formula directly. For $r \to r + 1$, $M(r + 1, t) = 2 M(r, t) + 1 \equiv 2 \cdot 2 + 1 \equiv 2 \pmod 3$, and $[M(r, t) \not\equiv 2 \pmod 3] = 0$, so each $(y, b)$ maps to $(2y + 1, b)$, preserving the bit $j \bmod 2$ and advancing $r$ to $r + 1$.

- **Step 4:** For $r \ge 2$ and $1 \le j \le 2t$, define
  $$U_j(r) := 2^{2t + r - 1} - 2^{r - 1 + j} + 2^{r - 1} - 2,$$
  so $U_1(r) > U_2(r) > \dots > U_{2t}(r) = 2^{r - 1} - 2 \ge 0$ and $2 U_j(r) = U_j(r + 1) - 2$. We prove by induction on $r \ge 2$ that $S(n(r, t))$ is the disjoint union $B_1(r) \sqcup \dots \sqcup B_{2t}(r)$, where all elements have distinct even first coordinates $y$, such that:
  - for $1 \le j \le 2t - 1$, $|B_j(r)| = r$, every $(y, b) \in B_j(r)$ has $b = (j + 1) \bmod 2$ and $U_{j+1}(r) + 2 \le y \le U_j(r)$;
  - for $j = 2t$, $|B_{2t}(r)| = r - 1$, every $(y, b) \in B_{2t}(r)$ has $b = 1$ and $0 \le y \le U_{2t}(r)$.

  Since $n(r, t) = 2 M(r, t)$ and $M(r, t) \equiv 2 \pmod 3$, we have $[M(r, t) \not\equiv 0 \pmod 3] = 1$ and $[M(r, t) \not\equiv 2 \pmod 3] = 0$. By Step 3, the $S(M(r, t))$ branch of $S(n(r, t))$ is exactly
  $$P_1(r) = \bigl\{\bigl(U_j(r),\, (j + 1) \bmod 2\bigr) : 1 \le j \le 2t\bigr\},$$
  and the $S(M(r, t) - 1)$ branch is $P_2(r) = \{(2y, b) : (y, b) \in S(M(r, t) - 1)\}$.

  - *Base $r = 2$:* Here $U_j(2) = 2^{2t + 1} - 2^{j + 1}$ ($U_{2t}(2) = 0$) and $M(2, t) - 1 = 2^{2t} - 3 = 2m' + 1$ with $m' = 2^{2t - 1} - 2 \equiv 0 \not\equiv 2 \pmod 3$. Applying Step 2 ($k = 2t - 1$) and the odd rule ($c = 1$) gives $S(2^{2t} - 3) = \{(2^{2t} - 2^{j+1} + 1,\, (j + 1) \bmod 2) : 1 \le j \le 2t - 1\}$, so
    $$P_2(2) = \bigl\{\bigl(2^{2t+1} - 2^{j+2} + 2,\, (j + 1) \bmod 2\bigr) : 1 \le j \le 2t - 1\bigr\} = \bigl\{\bigl(U_{j+1}(2) + 2,\, (j + 1) \bmod 2\bigr) : 1 \le j \le 2t - 1\bigr\}.$$
    For each $1 \le j \le 2t - 1$, $U_j(2) - (U_{j+1}(2) + 2) = 2^{j+1} - 2 \ge 2 > 0$. Setting $B_j(2) := \{(U_j(2), (j+1)\bmod 2),\, (U_{j+1}(2)+2, (j+1)\bmod 2)\}$ for $1 \le j \le 2t - 1$ and $B_{2t}(2) := \{(0, 1)\}$ verifies the base case.

  - *Step $r \to r + 1$:* Observe that $M(r + 1, t) - 1 = 2 M(r, t) = n(r, t)$, so $P_2(r + 1) = \bigsqcup_{j=1}^{2t} (2 \cdot B_j(r))$, where $2 \cdot B_j(r) := \{(2y, b) : (y, b) \in B_j(r)\}$. Set $B_j(r + 1) := \{(U_j(r + 1), (j + 1) \bmod 2)\} \cup (2 \cdot B_j(r))$. For $1 \le j \le 2t - 1$ and every $(y, b) \in B_j(r)$, the induction hypothesis gives $U_{j+1}(r) + 2 \le y \le U_j(r)$, whence
    $$U_{j+1}(r + 1) + 2 = 2 U_{j+1}(r) + 4 \le 2y \le 2 U_j(r) = U_j(r + 1) - 2 < U_j(r + 1).$$
    Hence $U_j(r + 1)$ is strictly larger than all first coordinates in $2 \cdot B_j(r)$, giving $|B_j(r + 1)| = r + 1$ distinct even elements in $[U_{j+1}(r + 1) + 2, U_j(r + 1)]$ with bit $(j + 1) \bmod 2$. For $j = 2t$, every $(y, b) \in B_{2t}(r)$ satisfies $0 \le 2y \le 2 U_{2t}(r) = U_{2t}(r + 1) - 2 < U_{2t}(r + 1)$, giving $|B_{2t}(r + 1)| = r$ distinct even elements in $[0, U_{2t}(r + 1)]$ with bit $1$.

  Since the intervals $[U_{j+1}(r) + 2, U_j(r)]$ ($1 \le j \le 2t - 1$) and $[0, U_{2t}(r)]$ are pairwise disjoint and strictly decreasing in $j$, sorting $S(n(r, t))$ by descending $y$ lists $B_1(r), B_2(r), \dots, B_{2t}(r)$ in order, yielding $V(n(r, t)) = (0^r 1^r)^{t - 1} 0^r 1^{r - 1}$.

**Verification from `A000361`.** Tracking $(\mathrm{fusc}(m), \mathrm{fusc}(m+1))$ under $m \mapsto 2m$ ($(p, q) \mapsto (p, p + q)$) and $m \mapsto 2m + 1$ ($(p, q) \mapsto (p + q, q)$) from $m = 1 \mapsto (1, 1)$ gives: $2^{2t-1} - 1 \mapsto (2t - 1, 1)$, $2^{2t} - 2 \mapsto (2t - 1, 2t)$, $M(r, t) \mapsto (2tr - 2t - 1, 2t)$ (after $r - 2$ odd steps), and $n(r, t) = 2 M(r, t) \mapsto (p_0, q_0) = (2tr - 2t - 1,\, 2tr - 1)$. Since $p_0$ is odd, $1 - p_0$ is even, so $v_j = ((j p_0 - 1) \bmod q_0) \bmod 2$. Writing $0 \le j < q_0 = 2tr - 1$ as $j = k r + i$ with $0 \le k \le 2t - 1$ and $0 \le i < r$ ($0 \le i < r - 1$ if $k = 2t - 1$), we have $j p_0 - 1 = j(q_0 - 2t) - 1 \equiv -k(q_0 + 1) - 2ti - 1 \equiv -(k + 2ti + 1) \pmod{q_0}$. Here $1 \le k + 2ti + 1 \le q_0$, with equality solely at $(k, i) = (2t - 2, r - 1)$ where $0 \equiv k \pmod 2$; for all other $(k, i)$, $(-(k + 2ti + 1)) \bmod q_0 = q_0 - k - 2ti - 1 = 2t(r - i) - 2 - k \equiv k \pmod 2$. Thus $v_{kr+i} = k \bmod 2$, again giving $(0^r 1^r)^{t-1} 0^r 1^{r-1}$. $\square$

## Lemma 2: Matrix Evaluation and Quadratic-Form Divisibility Theorem

Define the integer matrices
$$B := \begin{pmatrix} r & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad A := B^2 = \begin{pmatrix} r^2 + 1 & r \\ r & 1 \end{pmatrix},$$
and for $t \ge 0$, define $(x_t, y_t)^T := A^t (1, 1)^T$. Since $A \equiv I_2 \pmod r$, we have $x_t \equiv y_t \equiv 1 \pmod r$, so
$$X_t := \frac{x_t - 1}{r}, \qquad Y_t := \frac{y_t - 1}{r}, \qquad Z_t := Y_t + t$$
are integers (strictly positive for $r \ge 2, t \ge 1$).

**Lemma 2.** *For all integers $r \ge 2$ and $t \ge 1$:*
1. $a\bigl(n(r, t)\bigr) = r \cdot \gcd(X_t, Z_t)$.
2. $X_t \bigl(2 + r X_t - r - r^2 Y_t\bigr) + Z_t \bigl(-r - 2 - r Z_t + 2 r t\bigr) = t \bigl((t - 1) r - 2\bigr)$.
3. *Consequently,*
   $$\gcd(X_t, Z_t) \;\Big|\; t \cdot \bigl((t - 1) r - 2\bigr).$$

### Proof

Let $L = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $R = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ represent the actions of reading a `0` and a `1`, respectively. Then $R^r L^r = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & r \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ r & 1 \end{pmatrix} = A$. By Lemma 1, reading $V(n(r, t)) = (0^r 1^r)^{t-1} 0^r 1^{r-1}$ from left to right yields
$$\begin{pmatrix} p_{n(r,t)} \\ q_{n(r,t)} \end{pmatrix} = R^{r - 1} L^r A^{t - 1} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = R^{-1} A^t \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x_t \\ y_t \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} x_t - y_t \\ y_t \end{pmatrix}.$$
With $e_{n(r,t)} = tr$ and $f_{n(r,t)} = tr - 1$, we have
$$a\bigl(n(r, t)\bigr) = \gcd(x_t - y_t - t r,\, y_t + t r - 1) = \gcd(x_t - 1,\, y_t + t r - 1) = \gcd(r X_t,\, r Z_t) = r \gcd(X_t, Z_t),$$
proving (1).

For (2), consider the binary quadratic form $Q(x, y) := x^2 - r x y - y^2$. Direct expansion shows
$$Q\bigl(B(x, y)\bigr) = (r x + y)^2 - r(r x + y) x - x^2 = -x^2 + r x y + y^2 = -Q(x, y).$$
Hence $Q(A(x, y)) = Q(B^2(x, y)) = Q(x, y)$, so by induction on $t \ge 0$,
$$x_t^2 - r x_t y_t - y_t^2 = Q(x_t, y_t) = Q(1, 1) = -r.$$
Substituting $x_t = 1 + r X_t$ and $y_t = 1 + r Y_t$ gives
$$(1 + 2r X_t + r^2 X_t^2) - r(1 + r X_t + r Y_t + r^2 X_t Y_t) - (1 + 2r Y_t + r^2 Y_t^2) = -r.$$
Cancelling $1 - 1$ and $-r$ on both sides and dividing by $r$ yields
$$2 X_t + r X_t^2 - r X_t - r^2 X_t Y_t - (r + 2) Y_t - r Y_t^2 = 0.$$
Substituting $Y_t = Z_t - t$ in the last two terms gives
$$X_t(2 + r X_t - r - r^2 Y_t) - (r + 2)(Z_t - t) - r(Z_t^2 - 2 t Z_t + t^2) = 0,$$
which rearranges to
$$X_t(2 + r X_t - r - r^2 Y_t) + Z_t(-r - 2 - r Z_t + 2 r t) = r t^2 - r t - 2 t = t\bigl((t - 1) r - 2\bigr).$$
Statement (3) follows immediately since the left-hand side is an integer linear combination of $X_t$ and $Z_t$. $\square$

## Lemma 3: Elementary Cases ($r = 1$, Evens, and Odds $r \not\equiv 27 \pmod{30}$)

**Lemma 3.** *Every positive integer $r \ge 1$ with $r \not\equiv 27 \pmod{30}$ occurs infinitely often in $\mathrm{A400888}$.*

### Proof

By Lemma 1(1), it suffices to find a single index $n$ with $a(n) = r$.

1. **$r = 1$:** For $n = 2$, $w(2) = [5, 0]$, so $V(2) = (1, 0)$, $(p_2, q_2) = (2, 3)$, $e_2 = f_2 = 1$, and $a(2) = \gcd(2 - 1, 3 + 1) = 1$.
2. **Even $r \ge 2$:** Take $t = 1$. Then $(x_1, y_1) = (r^2 + r + 1,\, r + 1)$, so $X_1 = r + 1$ and $Z_1 = 2$. Since $r$ is even, $r + 1$ is odd, so $\gcd(X_1, Z_1) = 1$ and $a(n(r, 1)) = r$.
3. **Odd $r \ge 3$ with $r \not\equiv 2 \pmod 5$:** Take $t = 2$. Direct multiplication gives
   $$X_2 = r^3 + r^2 + 3r + 2, \qquad Z_2 = r^2 + r + 4.$$
   Since $X_2 - r Z_2 = 2 - r$ and $Z_2 = (r - 2)(r + 3) + 10$, we obtain
   $$\gcd(X_2, Z_2) = \gcd(r - 2,\, 10) = \gcd(r - 2,\, 5) = 1,$$
   as $r$ is odd and $r \not\equiv 2 \pmod 5$. Hence $a(n(r, 2)) = r$.
4. **Odd $r \ge 3$ with $r \equiv 2 \pmod 5$ and $3 \nmid r$:** Take $t = 3$. Direct multiplication gives
   $$X_3 = r^5 + r^4 + 5 r^3 + 4 r^2 + 6 r + 3, \qquad Z_3 = r^4 + r^3 + 4 r^2 + 3 r + 6.$$
   By Lemma 2(3), $\gcd(X_3, Z_3) \mid 3(2r - 2) = 6(r - 1)$.
   - Since $r$ is odd, $Z_3 \equiv 1 + 1 + 0 + 1 + 0 = 3 \equiv 1 \pmod 2$, so $2 \nmid \gcd(X_3, Z_3)$.
   - Modulo $3$, $X_3 \equiv r^5 + r^4 + 2 r^3 + r^2 = r^2(r^3 + r^2 + 2r + 1) \equiv r^2(r + 1 + 2r + 1) \equiv 2 r^2 \pmod 3$ (by Fermat's Little Theorem $r^3 \equiv r \pmod 3$ and $r^2 \equiv 1 \pmod 3$ for $3 \nmid r$). Since $3 \nmid r$, $3 \nmid X_3$.
   - If a prime $p$ divides $\gcd(X_3, Z_3)$ and $r - 1$, then $r \equiv 1 \pmod p$, so $p \mid \gcd(X_3(1), Z_3(1)) = \gcd(20, 15) = 5$, forcing $r \equiv 1 \pmod 5$, contradicting $r \equiv 2 \pmod 5$.

   Therefore $\gcd(X_3, Z_3) = 1$, and $a(n(r, 3)) = r$.

The only integers $r \ge 1$ not covered by cases 1–4 are odd integers with $r \equiv 2 \pmod 5$ and $3 \mid r$, i.e., $r \equiv 27 \pmod{30}$. $\square$

## Lemma 4: Parity Lemma

**Lemma 4.** *For every odd integer $r \ge 1$ and every $t \equiv 2 \pmod 3$, $X_t$ is odd; in particular $2 \nmid \gcd(X_t, Z_t)$.*

### Proof

Modulo $2$, $B \equiv \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, whose powers satisfy $B^2 \equiv \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B^3 \equiv I_2 \pmod 2$. Since $t \equiv 2 \pmod 3$, we have $2t \equiv 1 \pmod 3$, so
$$\begin{pmatrix} x_t \\ y_t \end{pmatrix} = B^{2t} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \equiv B \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \equiv \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \pmod 2.$$
Hence $x_t$ is even, $r X_t = x_t - 1$ is odd, and therefore $X_t$ is odd. $\square$

## Lemma 5: Algebraic Norm Bound Ruling Out $p \mid \gcd(X_t, Z_t)$

**Lemma 5.** *Fix an odd integer $r \ge 3$. There exists a constant $P_0(r) > r(r^2 + 4)$, depending only on $r$, such that whenever $t \ge 2$ and $p > P_0(r)$ is a prime satisfying $(t - 1) r - 2 = 2 p$, we have $p \nmid \gcd(X_t, Z_t)$.*

### Proof

Let $\alpha := \frac{r + \sqrt{r^2 + 4}}{2} > 1$ and $\beta := \frac{r - \sqrt{r^2 + 4}}{2} = -\alpha^{-1} \in (-1, 0)$ be the real roots of $\lambda^2 - r\lambda - 1 = 0$. For any integer $m \ge 1$, reduce $\gamma_m := \alpha^m (\alpha + 1)^r - (1 - \alpha)^r \in \mathbb{Z}[\alpha]$ via $\alpha^2 = r \alpha + 1$ to write $\gamma_m = u_m + v_m \alpha$ with $u_m, v_m \in \mathbb{Z}$, and let $\gamma'_m := \beta^m (\beta + 1)^r - (1 - \beta)^r = u_m + v_m \beta$. Their product
$$C_m(r) := \gamma_m \gamma'_m = (u_m + v_m \alpha)(u_m + v_m \beta) = u_m^2 + r u_m v_m - v_m^2$$
is an integer. Moreover, $C_m(r) \neq 0$:
- since $\alpha > 1$ and $m \ge 1$, we have $\alpha^m (\alpha + 1)^r > (\alpha + 1)^r > (\alpha - 1)^r = |1 - \alpha|^r$, so $\gamma_m > 0$;
- since $-1 < \beta < 0$ and $m \ge 1$, we have $|\beta^m (\beta + 1)^r| < 1 < (1 - \beta)^r$, so $\gamma'_m < 0$.

Thus $C_m(r) < 0$ is a nonzero integer for every $m \ge 1$. Define
$$P_0(r) := \max\bigl(r(r^2 + 4),\, |C_{3r + 8}(r)|,\, |C_{3r}(r)|\bigr).$$

Now let $p > P_0(r)$ be a prime with $(t - 1) r - 2 = 2 p$, so $t r = 2p + r + 2$ and $r(2t + 1) = 4p + 3r + 4$, and suppose for contradiction that $p \mid \gcd(X_t, Z_t)$. Then $x_t = 1 + r X_t \equiv 1 \pmod p$ and
$$y_t = 1 + r Y_t = 1 + r(Z_t - t) \equiv 1 - t r = 1 - (2p + r + 2) \equiv -r - 1 \pmod p.$$
Multiplying by $B = \begin{pmatrix} r & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ yields
$$B^{2t + 1} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = B \begin{pmatrix} x_t \\ y_t \end{pmatrix} \equiv \begin{pmatrix} r(1) + (-r - 1) \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \pmod p.$$

Since $p > r(r^2 + 4)$, we have $p \nmid 2$, $p \nmid r$, and $p \nmid (r^2 + 4)$. Hence $\lambda^2 - r\lambda - 1 = 0$ has two distinct roots $\bar{\alpha}, \bar{\beta} \in \mathbb{F}_{p^2}^\times$ with $\bar{\alpha} + \bar{\beta} = r$ and $\bar{\alpha} \bar{\beta} = -1$. Note $\bar{\alpha}, \bar{\beta} \neq \pm 1$ (since $(\pm 1)^2 - r(\pm 1) - 1 = \mp r \not\equiv 0 \pmod p$). In the eigenbasis $v_{\bar{\alpha}} = (\bar{\alpha}, 1)^T$, $v_{\bar{\beta}} = (\bar{\beta}, 1)^T$ of $B$ over $\mathbb{F}_{p^2}$,
$$\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \frac{1 - \bar{\beta}}{\bar{\alpha} - \bar{\beta}} v_{\bar{\alpha}} + \frac{\bar{\alpha} - 1}{\bar{\alpha} - \bar{\beta}} v_{\bar{\beta}}, \qquad \begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \frac{-1 - \bar{\beta}}{\bar{\alpha} - \bar{\beta}} v_{\bar{\alpha}} + \frac{\bar{\alpha} + 1}{\bar{\alpha} - \bar{\beta}} v_{\bar{\beta}}.$$
Equating $v_{\bar{\alpha}}$-coordinates in $B^{2t + 1}(1, 1)^T = (-1, 1)^T$ gives $\bar{\alpha}^{2t + 1}(1 - \bar{\beta}) = -1 - \bar{\beta}$. Multiplying by $\bar{\alpha}$ and using $\bar{\alpha}\bar{\beta} = -1$ gives
$$\bar{\alpha}^{2t + 1}(\bar{\alpha} + 1) = 1 - \bar{\alpha} \qquad \text{in } \mathbb{F}_{p^2}.$$
Raising both sides to the $r$-th power and substituting $r(2t + 1) = 4p + 3r + 4$ gives
$$\bar{\alpha}^{4p + 3r + 4}(\bar{\alpha} + 1)^r = (1 - \bar{\alpha})^r \qquad \text{in } \mathbb{F}_{p^2}.$$
We distinguish the two values of the Legendre symbol $\left(\frac{r^2 + 4}{p}\right) \in \{+1, -1\}$:
- If $\left(\frac{r^2 + 4}{p}\right) = +1$, then $\bar{\alpha} \in \mathbb{F}_p^\times$, so $\bar{\alpha}^p = \bar{\alpha}$ and $\bar{\alpha}^{4p + 3r + 4} = \bar{\alpha}^{3r + 8}$. Set $m := 3r + 8$.
- If $\left(\frac{r^2 + 4}{p}\right) = -1$, then Frobenius swaps the two roots ($\bar{\alpha}^p = \bar{\beta} = -\bar{\alpha}^{-1}$), so $\bar{\alpha}^{4p} = \bar{\alpha}^{-4}$ and $\bar{\alpha}^{4p + 3r + 4} = \bar{\alpha}^{3r}$. Set $m := 3r$.

In either case, the ring homomorphism $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha] \to \mathbb{F}_{p^2}$ sending $\alpha \mapsto \bar{\alpha}$ maps $\gamma_m = u_m + v_m \alpha$ to $\bar{\alpha}^m(\bar{\alpha} + 1)^r - (1 - \bar{\alpha})^r = 0$. Therefore
$$C_m(r) = u_m^2 + r u_m v_m - v_m^2 \equiv (u_m + v_m \bar{\alpha})(u_m + v_m \bar{\beta}) = 0 \pmod p,$$
so $p \mid C_m(r)$. Since $C_m(r) \neq 0$, this contradicts $p > P_0(r) \ge |C_m(r)|$. $\square$

## Lemma 6: Infinitely Many Solutions for Every Odd $r \ge 3$ with $3 \mid r$

**Lemma 6.** *Let $r \ge 3$ be an odd integer divisible by $3$. Then there exist infinitely many integers $t \ge 3$ such that $\gcd(X_t, Z_t) = 1$, and hence infinitely many indices $n(r, t)$ with $a(n(r, t)) = r$.*

### Proof

Define the integer sequence $F_0 = 0$, $F_1 = 1$, $F_{k + 1} = r F_k + F_{k - 1}$ ($k \ge 1$), so $B^k = \begin{pmatrix} F_{k + 1} & F_k \\ F_k & F_{k - 1} \end{pmatrix}$ for all $k \ge 1$. Since $F_1 = 1 < r + 1$, $F_2 = r < (r + 1)^2$, and $F_{k+1} = r F_k + F_{k-1} < (r + 1) F_k$ for $k \ge 2$, induction gives
$$0 < F_k < (r + 1)^k \qquad \text{for all } k \ge 1.$$

**Step 1 (Order $d_q$ modulo a prime $q > r(r^2 + 4)$).** For any prime $q > r(r^2 + 4)$, the eigenvalues $\bar{\alpha}, \bar{\beta} \in \mathbb{F}_{q^2}^\times$ of $B \bmod q$ are distinct, satisfy $\bar{\alpha}\bar{\beta} = -1$, and satisfy $\bar{\alpha}, \bar{\beta} \neq \pm 1$. Thus $\bar{\alpha}^2 \neq 1$ and $\bar{\beta}^2 = \bar{\alpha}^{-2}$ share the same multiplicative order
$$d_q := \mathrm{ord}_{\mathbb{F}_{q^2}^\times}(\bar{\alpha}^2) \ge 2.$$
We note three properties of $d_q$:
1. Since $d_q \mid |\mathbb{F}_{q^2}^\times| = q^2 - 1$, we have $\gcd(q, d_q) = 1$.
2. Since $A^{d_q} = B^{2 d_q} \equiv I_2 \pmod q$, its off-diagonal entry satisfies $q \mid F_{2 d_q}$.
3. If $q \mid t$ and $q \mid \gcd(X_t, Z_t)$, then $X_t \equiv Y_t = Z_t - t \equiv 0 \pmod q$, so $x_t \equiv y_t \equiv 1 \pmod q$, i.e., $A^t (1, 1)^T \equiv (1, 1)^T \pmod q$. Because both eigenbasis coefficients $(1 - \bar{\beta})/(\bar{\alpha} - \bar{\beta})$ and $(\bar{\alpha} - 1)/(\bar{\alpha} - \bar{\beta})$ of $(1, 1)^T$ are nonzero in $\mathbb{F}_{q^2}$, it follows that $\bar{\alpha}^{2t} = 1$, hence $d_q \mid t$. Since $\gcd(q, d_q) = 1$, this yields $q d_q \mid t$; moreover, if $t$ is odd, then $d_q$ must be odd.

**Step 2 (Convergent tail bound).** For any integer $Y > r(r^2 + 4)$, define
$$S(Y) := \sum_{\substack{q > Y \text{ prime} \\ d_q \text{ odd}}} \frac{1}{\varphi(q d_q)} = \sum_{\substack{q > Y \text{ prime} \\ d_q \text{ odd}}} \frac{1}{(q - 1)\varphi(d_q)}.$$
We prove that $\lim_{Y \to \infty} S(Y) = 0$. Partition the primes $q > Y$ with $d_q$ odd into $\mathcal{A} := \{q : d_q \ge (\log q)^4\}$ and $\mathcal{B} := \{q : d_q < (\log q)^4\}$.
- *Sum over $\mathcal{A}$:* For every odd prime power $\ell^k$ ($\ell \ge 3, k \ge 1$), $\varphi(\ell^k) / \sqrt{\ell^k} = \ell^{(k - 1)/2}(\ell - 1)/\sqrt{\ell} \ge 2/\sqrt{3} > 1$; multiplying over prime powers gives $\varphi(d) \ge \sqrt{d}$ for every odd integer $d \ge 1$. Since $q > Y > 39$, we have $q - 1 \ge q / 2$ and $\varphi(d_q) \ge (\log q)^2$, whence
  $$\sum_{q \in \mathcal{A}} \frac{1}{(q - 1)\varphi(d_q)} \le 2 \sum_{n = Y + 1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n (\log n)^2} \le 2 \int_{Y}^{\infty} \frac{dx}{x (\log x)^2} = \frac{2}{\log Y}.$$
- *Sum over $\mathcal{B}$:* Each $q \in \mathcal{B}$ has odd $d = d_q \ge 3$, satisfies $q > \max(Y, e^{d^{1/4}})$ (so $q - 1 \ge \frac{1}{2}\max(Y, e^{d^{1/4}})$), and divides $F_{2d}$. Since $0 < F_{2d} < (r + 1)^{2d}$, the number of distinct prime divisors of $F_{2d}$ is at most $\log_2 F_{2d} < 2 d \log_2(r + 1)$. Using $\varphi(d) \ge 1$ and setting $D_0 := \lfloor (\log Y)^4 \rfloor$,
  $$\sum_{q \in \mathcal{B}} \frac{1}{(q - 1)\varphi(d_q)} \le \sum_{d = 3}^{\infty} \frac{4 d \log_2(r + 1)}{\max(Y, e^{d^{1/4}})} \le \frac{4 \log_2(r + 1) (\log Y)^8}{Y} + 4 \log_2(r + 1) \sum_{d > D_0} d \, e^{-d^{1/4}}.$$
  Because $e^u > u^9 / 9!$ for all $u > 0$, we have $d e^{-d^{1/4}} < 9! / d^{5/4}$, so $\sum_{d = 3}^{\infty} d e^{-d^{1/4}} < \infty$ and its tail tends to $0$ as $Y \to \infty$.

Combining the bounds for $\mathcal{A}$ and $\mathcal{B}$ gives $\lim_{Y \to \infty} S(Y) = 0$.

**Step 3 (Choice of arithmetic progression).** Fix an integer $Y > r(r^2 + 4)$ large enough that
$$S(Y) < \frac{1}{20},$$
and set $W := 6 \prod_{5 \le \ell \le Y,\, \ell \text{ prime}} \ell$. By the Chinese Remainder Theorem, let $u_0 \in \{0, \dots, W - 1\}$ satisfy
$$u_0 \equiv 0 \pmod 2, \qquad u_0 \equiv 2 \pmod 3, \qquad u_0 \equiv 0 \pmod{\ell} \quad \text{for every prime } 5 \le \ell \le Y,$$
and set $a_0 := (r u_0 - 1) \bmod (r W)$. We check $\gcd(a_0, r W) = 1$: since $r$ is odd and $u_0$ is even, $r u_0 - 1$ is odd; since $3 \mid r$, $r u_0 - 1 \equiv -1 \pmod 3$; for every prime $5 \le \ell \le Y$ (which includes all prime divisors of $r$ since $Y > r$), $u_0 \equiv 0 \pmod{\ell}$ gives $r u_0 - 1 \equiv -1 \pmod{\ell}$.

For any prime $p \le X$ with $p \equiv a_0 \pmod{r W}$ and $p > P_0(r)$:
- $u := (p + 1) / r \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1}$ satisfies $u \equiv u_0 \pmod W$;
- $t := 2u + 1 = (2p + r + 2)/r \ge 3$ satisfies $(t - 1) r - 2 = 2p$ and $t \equiv 2 u_0 + 1 \equiv 5 \equiv 2 \pmod 3$;
- $\gcd(t, r W) = 1$, because $t = 2u + 1$ is odd, $t \equiv 2 \pmod 3$, and $t \equiv 1 \pmod{\ell}$ for every prime $5 \le \ell \le Y$;
- for $X \ge 6$ (using $r \ge 3$), $t = (2p + r + 2)/r \le \frac{2}{3} X + \frac{5}{3} \le X$.

By Lemma 2(3), Lemma 4 ($t \equiv 2 \pmod 3 \implies 2 \nmid \gcd(X_t, Z_t)$), and Lemma 5 ($p > P_0(r) \implies p \nmid \gcd(X_t, Z_t)$), any such prime $p$ with $\gcd(X_t, Z_t) > 1$ must have some prime divisor $q \mid t$ dividing $\gcd(X_t, Z_t)$. By $\gcd(t, r W) = 1$ and Step 1, this prime $q$ satisfies
$$q > Y > r(r^2 + 4), \qquad d_q \text{ odd}, \qquad \gcd(q d_q, r W) = 1, \qquad q d_q \mid t \le X.$$

**Step 4 (Counting good primes via PNT in AP and Brun–Titchmarsh).** Take $X \ge \max(6, (r W)^4)$ and partition the bad primes $p \in (P_0(r), X]$ with $p \equiv a_0 \pmod{r W}$ according to the size of $q d_q$ and $d_q$:

1. *Case 1 ($q d_q \le X^{1/2}$):* Fix a prime $q > Y$ with $d_q$ odd, $\gcd(q d_q, r W) = 1$, and $q d_q \le X^{1/2}$. Since $q d_q$ is odd, $q d_q \mid (2u + 1)$ fixes a unique residue class $u \equiv u_q \pmod{q d_q}$. By CRT (as $\gcd(q d_q, W) = 1$), combining with $u \equiv u_0 \pmod W$ determines a unique residue class $p = r u - 1 \equiv a_q \pmod{M_q}$, where $M_q := r W q d_q$. We have $\gcd(a_q, M_q) = 1$: indeed $\gcd(a_q, r W) = \gcd(a_0, r W) = 1$, and $2 a_q + r + 2 \equiv r(2 u_q + 1) \equiv 0 \pmod{q d_q}$, so any prime $\ell \mid q d_q$ dividing $a_q$ would divide $r + 2$, contradicting $\ell > Y > r(r^2 + 4) > r + 2$ (as $\gcd(q d_q, W) = 1$). Since $M_q = r W q d_q \le X^{1/4} \cdot X^{1/2} = X^{3/4} < X$, the Brun–Titchmarsh inequality (Montgomery–Vaughan, *Mathematika* **20** (1973), 119–134) gives
   $$\pi(X; M_q, a_q) < \frac{2 X}{\varphi(M_q) \log(X / M_q)} \le \frac{2 X}{\varphi(r W)\varphi(q d_q) \cdot \frac{1}{4}\log X} = \frac{8 X}{\varphi(r W)\varphi(q d_q)\log X}.$$
   Summing over all such $q$ yields
   $$E_1(X) \le \frac{8 X}{\varphi(r W)\log X} \cdot S(Y) < \frac{2}{5} \cdot \frac{X}{\varphi(r W)\log X}.$$

2. *Case 2A ($X^{1/2} < q d_q \le X$ and $d_q \le X^{1/5}$):* The map $p \mapsto t = (2p + r + 2)/r$ injects into $\{1, \dots, \lfloor X \rfloor\}$. For each integer $2 \le d \le \lfloor X^{1/5} \rfloor$, at most $\log_2 F_{2d} < 2 d \log_2(r + 1)$ primes $q$ divide $F_{2d}$, and for each such $q$ with $q d > X^{1/2}$, the number of multiples $t \in [1, X]$ of $q d$ is $\lfloor X / (q d) \rfloor < X^{1/2}$. Hence
   $$E_{2A}(X) \le \sum_{d = 2}^{\lfloor X^{1/5} \rfloor} 2 d \log_2(r + 1) \cdot X^{1/2} \le 2 \log_2(r + 1)\, X^{2/5} \cdot X^{1/2} = 2 \log_2(r + 1)\, X^{9/10}.$$

3. *Case 2B ($X^{1/2} < q d_q \le X$ and $d_q > X^{1/5}$):* Every such prime satisfies $q \le X / d_q < X^{4/5}$, and the number of positive multiples $t \le X$ of $q d_q$ is $\lfloor X / (q d_q) \rfloor \le X^{4/5} / q$. Hence
   $$E_{2B}(X) \le X^{4/5} \sum_{q < X^{4/5}} \frac{1}{q} \le X^{4/5} \sum_{n = 1}^{\lfloor X^{4/5} \rfloor} \frac{1}{n} \le X^{4/5}(1 + \log X).$$

Since $r W$ is a fixed modulus (depending only on $r$) and $\gcd(a_0, r W) = 1$, the Prime Number Theorem in Arithmetic Progressions gives $\pi(X; r W, a_0) \sim \frac{X}{\varphi(r W) \log X}$ as $X \to \infty$; thus for all sufficiently large $X$,
$$\pi(X; r W, a_0) \ge \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{X}{\varphi(r W) \log X}.$$
Therefore the number of primes $p \in (P_0(r), X]$ with $p \equiv a_0 \pmod{r W}$ for which $\gcd(X_{t(p)}, Z_{t(p)}) = 1$ is at least
$$\pi(X; r W, a_0) - P_0(r) - E_1(X) - E_{2A}(X) - E_{2B}(X) \ge \frac{1}{10} \cdot \frac{X}{\varphi(r W)\log X} - 2 \log_2(r + 1)\, X^{9/10} - X^{4/5}(1 + \log X) - P_0(r),$$
which diverges to $+\infty$ as $X \to \infty$. Because $p \mapsto t(p) \mapsto n(r, t(p))$ is strictly increasing, Lemma 2(1) yields infinitely many indices $n(r, t)$ with $a(n(r, t)) = r$. $\square$

## Proof of the Main Theorem

Combining Lemma 3 (covering $r = 1$, all even $r \ge 2$, and all odd $r \ge 3$ with $r \not\equiv 27 \pmod{30}$) with Lemma 6 (covering all odd $r \ge 3$ with $3 \mid r$, in particular all $r \equiv 27 \pmod{30}$), every positive integer $r \ge 1$ occurs infinitely many times in OEIS `A400888`. $\square$
